Tuning the jump

A good platformer jump is built from about a dozen numbers. Here are mine, why each one exists, and how I found them.

When I started Mousecapades, the brief was one sentence: it has to feel amazing to play. You can't write "feels amazing" into code, so the first thing we built wasn't a level. It was a tuning panel: a sidebar of sliders wired straight into the physics, so I could change a value mid-jump and feel the difference immediately.

Units first

Everything runs at a fixed 60 steps per second, on a 320 × 180 canvas with 16-pixel tiles. Speeds are in pixels per frame, so "2.5" means 2.5 pixels every sixtieth of a second. Small, readable numbers make tuning a conversation instead of guesswork.

Running: three speeds and a skid

  • Walk 1.5, run 2.5, sprint 3.25. Hold run and you reach 2.5. Keep running flat out and a six-segment meter fills, and once it's full you can hit 3.25.
  • Acceleration 0.09. It takes about half a second to reach running speed. Fast enough to feel responsive, slow enough to feel like weight.
  • Friction 0.07, skid 0.25. Let go and she coasts. Push the other way and she digs in more than three times harder. That difference is what makes turning around feel deliberate.

Jumping: two gravities

The trick behind "hold to jump higher" is that gravity changes. While you hold the button on the way up, gravity is a light 0.17. Let go, or start falling, and it jumps to 0.5.

  • Launch speed 5.0 gives a full jump of 72 pixels, four and a half tiles. A quick tap gives about 26.
  • Run bonus 0.2: every pixel per frame of running speed adds to the launch, so a sprinting jump clears around six tiles high and twelve across.
  • Apex hang: near the top of the jump gravity is cut a little more, which gives a beat of air to line up a landing.
  • Falling is faster than rising. The jump goes up floaty and comes down snappy, so landings feel decisive.

Forgiveness: the invisible part

  • Coyote time, 6 frames. Run off a ledge and you can still jump for a tenth of a second. Players think they jumped on time, and with this they did.
  • Jump buffer, 6 frames. Press jump just before you land and it fires the moment you touch down.
  • Corner nudge, 4 pixels. Clip the edge of a block with your head and you slide past it instead of bonking.
None of these are visible, and players never thank you for them. Without them, though, the controls feel unfair, and people blame themselves for the game's mistakes.

How I found the numbers

I built a test course where each stretch measures one thing: gaps of 3, 5 and 7 tiles, pillars of 3, 4 and 5 tiles, and a long runway to fill the sprint meter. I play a section, move one slider, and play it again. When something feels off, I try to name the exact moment, like "the turnaround is sluggish" or "it hangs too long at the top", because a precise note maps onto a single slider. The panel's "copy values" button then saves the result back into the game.

The AI wrote the physics. I decided what it should feel like. Splitting the work that way is the whole point of the panel.
